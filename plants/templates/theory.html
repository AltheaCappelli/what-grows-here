Data processing behind the plant suggestions

The suggestions in this webapp come from a combination of climate data, here USDA hardiness zones and mean daily sunlight hours, with the balcony direction. Together, these factors determine which plants are likely to thrive on a balcony at a given location.

The USDA hardiness zone classifies regions by average annual minimum winter temperatures. This indicates how resistant plants must be to cold in order to survive the local climate. The zones range from 1 (coldest) to 13 (warmest), with each zone representing about 5.6°C difference in the average annual minimum temperature. For example, zone 1 corresponds to temperatures below -45.6°C, while zone 13 includes areas with minimum temperatures above 15°C. Since I could not find a suitable high-resolution grid for Europe, I computed it myself, using themean winter temperatures from the Copernicus ERA5 reanalysis dataset. Specifically, I used the mean winter temperatures from 1991 to 2020, as winters have become noticeably warmer due to climate change, and assigned each grid point to the corresponding USDA zone.
The resulting map shows a good agreement compared to existing maps, which confirms the validity of my result.

Estimating the mean daily sunlight hours was more complex. Again, I could not find a pre-computed grid dataset, so I derived it myself. I used data from the Copernicus ERA5 reanalysis, specifically the surface solar radiation downwards variable (shortwave radiation at the surface). To translate this into actual sunshine hours, I used the FAO56 Shortwave Method , which gives a correlation between the amount of incoming solar radiation and the actual duration of sunshine hours. The core equation can be expressed as:

$$R_s = \bigl(a_s + b_s \cdot \tfrac{n}{N}\bigr)\,R_a, $$

where \(R_s\) is the surface shortwave radiation (MJ m⁻² day⁻¹), \(R_a\) is the extraterrestrial radiation (MJ m⁻² day⁻¹), \(a_s\) and \(b_s\) are empirical coefficients (their sum represents the fraction of extraterrestrial radiation reaching the earth on clear days and are estimated to be 0.25 and 0.50 respectively), \(n\) is the actual duration of sunshine (hours), and \(N\) is the maximum possible duration of sunshine or day length (hours).
By rearranging the formula to solve for \(n\), we get:

$$ n = \biggl(\frac{\bigl(\frac{R_s}{R_a}\bigr) - a_s}{b_s}\biggr)\,N. $$

The extraterrestrial radiation \(R_a\) itself depends on astronomical factors such as the solar constant \(G_{sc}\) (0.0820 MJ m⁻² min⁻¹), Earth–Sun distance \(d_r\), latitude \(\phi\) (rad), solar declination \(\delta\) (rad), and sunset hour angle \(\omega_s\) (rad). These are computed using standard formulas based on the day of the year:

$$ d_r = 1 + 0.033 \cdot \cos\left(\frac{2\pi J}{365}\right), $$ $$ \delta = 0.409 \cdot \sin\left(\frac{2\pi J}{365} - 1.39\right), $$ $$ \omega_s = \arccos(-\tan(\phi) \cdot \tan(\delta)). $$

The full equation for \(R_a\) is:

$$ R_a = \frac{24 \cdot 60}{\pi} \cdot G_{sc} \cdot d_r \cdot [\omega_s \cdot \sin(\phi) \cdot \sin(\delta) + \cos(\phi) \cdot \cos(\delta) \cdot \sin(\omega_s)]. $$

Finally, the maximum possible duration of sunshine \(N\) is given by:

$$ N = \frac{24}{\pi} \cdot \omega_s. $$

By computing these formulas in Python and applying them at each grid point, the values of mean daily sunshine hours for Europe could be computed. The resulting map provides a reasonable estimate of sunshine hours.

Self-computed mean daily sunshine hours map for Europe (1991–2020).

Lastly, the balcony direction (N, S, E, W) was also taken into account, as it influences the amount of sunlight a balcony receives throughout the day. On a northern balcony for example, shade-tolerant plants are recommended, while southern balconies can accommodate sun-loving plants. Both west and east facing balconies receive moderate sunlight, so a mix of plants with varying light needs can be suggested.
The final plant suggestions are therefore based on a combination of the USDA hardiness zone, mean daily sunlight hours, and balcony direction, ensuring that the recommended plants are likely to thrive in the specific conditions of each location.

Assumptions:

  • Reference period: 1991–2020, since it reflects recent climate taking into account effects of climate change.
  • Resolution: The gridded output map smooths out small-scale variations such as urban heat islands, courtyards, shading, etc.
  • Sunlight estimate: FAO-56 is an empirical method, so building shading, vegetation and other and small obstructions are not considered.
  • Plant data: The database gives general guidelines, so these should be taken as suggestions and not as strict rules. Microclimate, care, soil, pot size, etc. also play a role.

Code & data

The used climate data is from the Copernicus Climate Data Store.
The plants database is a combination of various sources such as Ponics life and Victory Seeds.
